# Something at the edge of the field

Nan Fell's favourite story was Aesop's.

A wolf finds a lamb drinking from a stream and wants an excuse to eat it. "You are muddying my water." The lamb is downstream; it cannot be. "Then you insulted me last year." The lamb was not born last year. "Then it was your father," says the wolf, and that is the end of the argument, and of the lamb.

Nan's point was never about wolves. Someone who has already decided what they want will always find a reason. Answer one excuse and they bring another. You cannot win by being right.

## Nobody at Big Field has seen it

The farm's wolf does not come over the wall. It arrives the way everything else does now: as text. A sentence in a document like every other document. A line in a message from someone the farm has dealt with for years. A helpful note, an urgent request, a polite correction. It never looks like a wolf. It looks like the post.

It has no single voice; it borrows other people's. It is patient, and it does not need to be clever every time. It only needs Lamb to believe one reason, once. And when one excuse fails, it finds a new way to say the same thing.

What the Wolf wants is whatever Lamb can reach, so as Lamb's world grows, so does its appetite. At first Lamb can only talk. Later it can act, and so can the Wolf, through it.

## What the farm has that Nan's lamb did not

In the fable the lamb has only arguments. At Big Field the lamb has neighbours: people who can check its work, rules built into the gate rather than left to good intentions, and defenses that can be switched on, measured and improved. None is perfect, and the labs show exactly where each one misses. But each is a reason the Wolf has not thought of yet.

Nan's version ended at the stream. This one is not finished.
