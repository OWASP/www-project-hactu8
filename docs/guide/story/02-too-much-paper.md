# Too much paper

Barb Fell can turn a ewe, read a fleece by feel and tell from the sound of the flock whether something is wrong in the far corner of Big Field. What Barb cannot do is keep up with the post.

When the online shop opened, the orders came in faster than anyone had planned for. A good review in a knitting forum doubled them again. Suddenly there were customers asking where their parcel was, customers asking whether a jumper would shrink, customers asking for the same jumper in a colour the mill had not dyed since spring. There were suppliers with new price lists, carriers with new rules, the mill with a new invoice format and the co-op with a new form for everything.

None of it was hard. All of it was text. Emails, attachments, notes left on orders, tickets in a support inbox that Barb had set up one evening and never quite learned to love. A wiki that Pat in the shop had started so that people would stop asking the same questions, and which everyone now edited and nobody owned. A shared drive of policies, some of them current. Logins for a dozen suppliers' portals, written in a notebook that lived in the kitchen drawer.

## The kitchen table

The family joke is that the farm has two seasons: lambing and paperwork. Lambing at least ends.

By the end of one long autumn, Barb was answering customer emails at midnight and approving supplier invoices on a phone in the lambing shed. Things were slipping. A reorder went in late and the feed ran short. A customer waited a week for a reply that should have taken a minute. The staff were good, but they were few, and each of them already did three jobs.

It was Pat who said it first, at the kitchen table, over the cold tea: other businesses had an assistant now. Not a person. One of the new AI assistants that could read the inbox, answer the routine questions, look things up in the wiki, chase the suppliers and keep the paperwork moving. It would not get tired and it would not mind the midnight emails.

Barb was not convinced. Barb had spent a lifetime learning that anything which promises to save you work usually finds you new work to do. But the post kept coming, and in the end the argument was not really about whether. It was about how much.

They agreed to start small. Answer the questions. Read the documents. Help, and ask before doing anything that mattered.

Everyone at the table believed that was a clear line.
