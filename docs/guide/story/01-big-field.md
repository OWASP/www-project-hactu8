# Big Field

The farm sits at the end of a lane too narrow for two vans. There is a yard, a stone house with a kitchen that has always been the office, a shearing shed with a tin roof that rings in the rain, and beyond them all the field the farm is named for.

Big Field is not big by any measure a surveyor would use. It earned the name because it was the first field the Fell family ever owned outright, and to the people who bought it, it felt enormous. It is bounded on three sides by a dry-stone wall that is a little lower than it ought to be, and that is where the rest of the name comes from.

## The jumpers

The ewes of Big Field jump the wall. Not all of them and not often, but often enough that the neighbours started calling them "the Big Field jumpers" long before anyone at the farm knit anything. Every few weeks someone would ring the house to say that two of Fell's ewes were on the road again, and someone would go out with a bucket and bring them home.

Nan Fell, who ran the farm for most of the last century, had a sense of humour about it. She also had a spinning wheel, a box of needles and more wool than the wool board would pay a fair price for. One winter she knitted a jumper with a ewe clearing a wall across the front, sold it at the gate for more than the fleece had fetched, and knitted another. When she painted a sign for the lane, it said **Big Field Jumpers**, and nobody was quite sure whether it meant the sheep or the knitwear. She preferred it that way.

## The company

The sign became a company when Barb Fell, Nan's grandchild, took over. Barb kept the flock, kept the name and made it official: Big Field Jumper Co. The wool now goes to a mill a few valleys over, the mill sends back yarn, and the yarn becomes jumpers, hats and socks that sell in a small farm shop by the gate and, increasingly, online.

Some things have not changed. The shop still has a bell over the door. The kitchen table is still where the decisions get made, usually over tea that has gone cold. And the ewes still jump the wall.

What has changed is everything around the edges. A family farm that sells knitwear is not really one business but several: a flock to keep, feed to buy, a vet to call, a mill to deal with, a co-op to stay friendly with, parcels to post, customers to answer and a shop to run. Each of those comes with its own people, its own paperwork and its own way of getting in touch.

That is where the trouble started. Not with a wolf. With paper.
