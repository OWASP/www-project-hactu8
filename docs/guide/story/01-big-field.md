# Big Field

The farm sits at the end of a lane too narrow for two vans: a yard, a stone house whose kitchen has always been the office, a shearing shed with a tin roof, and beyond them the field the farm is named for.

Big Field is not big. It was the first field the Fell family ever owned outright, and to them it felt enormous. Its dry-stone wall is a little lower than it ought to be, and that gave the farm the rest of its name.

## The jumpers

The ewes of Big Field jump the wall, often enough that the neighbours called them "the Big Field jumpers" long before anyone at the farm knitted anything.

Nan Fell, who ran the farm for most of the last century, also had a spinning wheel and more wool than the wool board would pay a fair price for. One winter she knitted a jumper with a ewe clearing a wall across the front, sold it at the gate, and knitted another. Her sign for the lane said **Big Field Jumpers**. Did it mean the sheep that jump the wall, the wool off their backs, or the jumpers (sweaters) the wool becomes? Nan's answer was yes. She liked a name that did three jobs, the way everyone at Big Field does.

## The company

Nan's grandchild, Barb Fell, made the name official: Big Field Jumpers Co. The wool goes to a mill a few valleys over, comes back as yarn, and leaves as jumpers, hats and socks, sold in the farm shop by the gate and, more and more, online.

The shop still has a bell over the door, decisions are still made at the kitchen table over cold tea, and the ewes still jump the wall. What has changed is everything around the edges. A family farm that sells knitwear is really several businesses: a flock, feed, a vet, a mill, a co-op, parcels, customers and a shop. Each comes with its own people and its own paperwork.

That is where the trouble started. Not with a wolf. With paper.
